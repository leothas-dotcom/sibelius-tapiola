## Kuvaus

An AI listened to a symphony orchestra and drew what it heard. Every frame of this film is code. No cameras, no AI-generated images, no video model.

The music is Tapiola (1926), Jean Sibelius' last great orchestral work: eighteen minutes inside the ancient northern forest of Tapio, the forest god of Finnish myth.

HOW IT WAS MADE

1. Listening. Claude wrote its own tools to measure the recording: when each phrase begins, how loud each group of instruments is, where the accents fall. Then it read the orchestral score to know who is actually playing.

2. A musician's ear. I play in an orchestra. I watched every draft and told Claude what I heard and what the picture was missing, close to two hundred notes in all. Claude went back to the recording and the score each time and measured again.

3. Every instrument is a force of nature. The violas are the wind that moves the falling snow. The timpani are a tremor in the ground. The brass is a red sun that gives no warmth. Nothing flickers along with a spectrum: the picture follows who is speaking in the music.

4. Sound is a force, not a position. The loudness of the strings pushes the snow the way wind does: it starts slowly, gathers speed and keeps drifting after the sound has ended. That one rule, F = ma, is what makes the motion feel real.

5. Drawing. The forest, the snow, the light and the northern lights are all computed in code from those measurements, frame by frame.

WHY

When I was a child, my father and I listened to records together, and he told me what was happening in the music: who speaks, who arrives. This film listens the way he taught me.
